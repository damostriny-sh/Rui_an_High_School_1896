## 1. What is fixed and what is open

**[Confirmed]**
- A school in bright, saturated, semi-realistic reality, and a grey consciousness space (Gray Space) that reality switches into under specific conditions. The switching drives the story.
- Doing a question is the fight. The question becomes a body; the solving method is the ability; the right method wins far more cheaply than force.
- Skipping costs health. Long academic decline costs sanity. Campus leisure restores both.
- Classes, homework, exams, meals and rest form the day.
- Theme: the absurd and the helpless, with one existential bright note. Neither "work hard and win" nor "nothing matters".
- The Gray Space is built from giant hands and limbs, impossible scale, and ordinary objects as focal points. In `sipping_mountain_front` the layered forms are limbs.
- Third person. The campus HUD design is retained as a design only.

**Open (undecided, and I do not decide them here)**
- Who the student is; every other character; all plot.
- What the hands, the star, the fish, the pencils and the furniture mean.
- Whether the fight is real-time or turn-based; all numbers.
- How many days, subjects and places the game has.
- What the Gray Space finally is.

## 2. Creative analysis

**2.1 The most distinctive idea.** The method is the verb. In almost every game about school, studying is a menu and fighting is something else. Here the act of solving is the fight itself, and the opponent's body is the shape of the question. Joined to the owner's drawings, this gives something I have not seen elsewhere: *a world where thinking is done with enormous hands*.

**2.2 Where the potential is.**
- The drawings contain no monsters. They contain hands, sleeves, a table, cups, pencils, a star, fish **[Observed]**. That is a gift: the game does not need a bestiary. It needs a vocabulary of gestures.
- The return. Coming back to the same bright room, slightly later, with the page changed, can carry more story than any cutscene.
- Recovery as "useless" life. This is where the bright note can live in the player's hands rather than in dialogue.

**2.3 The serious risks. These are disagreements with the concept as written, and I state them plainly.**

1. **The rules, as listed, argue for the opposite of the theme.** Right method wins cheaply; failure costs health; bad grades cost sanity; leisure refills both. Play that optimally and the game says: study well, rest efficiently, succeed. That is exactly the message the owner rejects. The system needs a rule under which doing well is *necessary and never sufficient*. See 2.5.
2. **"Right method = quick kill" collapses into lock and key.** If each question has one correct method and anything else is "wrong", the player is matching a label to a label. That is a quiz with an animation. Methods must be *operations that always do something true*, some of which help.
3. **Real questions exclude most players.** The more authentic the content, the smaller the audience. What must be authentic is the *reasoning*, not the syllabus.
4. **"Personified questions" invites mascots.** A cute or scary creature per question type would break from the drawings and cost a great deal. I propose that questions are never creatures (section 10).
5. **A full calendar of classes, homework, meals, rest, leisure and friendships is the structure of an existing famous series.** Copying its abundance of free time and its relationship meters would make this a school RPG. Here free time should be *scarce*, and nothing personal should have a meter.
6. **Sanity as a horror gauge is a cliché** (hallucinated monsters, screen distortion). It would also break the rule that reality stays real.
7. **Scale of production.** Walkable spaces made of giant anatomy are expensive. The number of Gray Space rooms must be small and each must earn its place.

**2.4 Keeping question-combat from being a gimmick.** Four rules:
- **The player never computes.** The player sees relations (the same thing twice, two things that oppose, a part inside a whole) and performs an operation. The world does the arithmetic and shows the result.
- **Every operation is legal and has a true result.** Subtracting where adding was neat gives a correct but longer road, exactly as on paper. Nothing answers "invalid".
- **There are always three ways through:** understand (cheap and decisive), force (works, costs body and time), leave (costs body, saves time, loses the mark). Choosing among them is the real decision.
- **The unit of design is the question type, not the question.** A type has a body, a behaviour and a weakness that follow from its structure. Instances of a type vary the numbers. Recognising a type the second time and ending it in one move is the game's version of learning.

**2.5 Making health, sanity and grades a real loop.**
- **Health is the body's share of the day.** It is spent in the Gray Space: by being struck, by forcing, and by leaving. *Why does skipping cost health?* Because a question does not put down what it is holding when you walk away. You can always leave, but you leave through the grip, and the blank stays on the page for the school to count. Skipping is therefore not shameful; it is triage with a price.
- **Grades are the only thing the school can see.** It sees the answer, never how you got it.
- **Rank is relative, and the cohort keeps improving.** The same performance sinks over a term. Holding a rank costs more hours each week. This is the treadmill: effort is required and does not settle anything. *This single rule is what makes the system absurd rather than merely hard.*
- **Sanity follows the long trend of evaluation,** never a single question.
- **Low sanity narrows the world to what is graded.** No monsters. Names become rank numbers. Colour thins in places with no academic use. The interaction highlight stops appearing for anything unscored, although the action still works if the player goes and does it. In the Gray Space, the hands crowd closer and there is less empty room to read in.
- **Recovery is paid for with the only scarce thing: a free slot.** Most of the day is not the player's. The few slots that are can go to more questions (grade) or to something the school does not count (health, sanity). The game never previews what an unscored act will give.

**2.6 Putting the absurd and the bright note into play, not into lines.**
- The absurd is produced by a *reasonable* system repeating: bells, slots, a rank that moves when you do not. Nobody is a villain and nothing needs to be strange.
- The bright note is a mechanical fact: the acts that keep the student going are the ones the game refuses to score, prompt strongly or praise. The player has to choose them without being told they were right.

**2.7 What the player should finally feel.** Not triumph and not despair. Something closer to: *I know exactly how this machine works, it will not stop, and I still decided what this afternoon was for.*

## 3. Creative thesis

> **Rui'an High School 1896 is a game about being measured.** By day you keep a timetable in a bright school that can count everything about you except that you are alive. Each time you face a question, the room falls away into a grey place made of enormous hands, where the question has a body and the only weapon is to see how it is put together. You cannot beat the school and the game will not let you. What it gives you is a few hours nobody counts, and it never tells you what to do with them.

## 4. Creative pillars

A design that fails a pillar is rejected.

| # | Pillar | What the player visibly experiences | Tradeoff it forces |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Understanding is the only weapon** | You look before you act. A fight you understand ends in one or two deliberate moves. Force works but hurts | No levelling, no stronger gear, no damage numbers. Encounters are short and hand-made, so there will be few of them |
| 2 | **The question has a body** | An opponent's shape, behaviour and weak point come from how the question is built. Every operation changes it truthfully | No generic enemies and no reskins. Each question *type* is a design job; the type count stays small |
| 3 | **Hands make the grey world** | Space is formed by giant limbs and gestures around one ordinary object. A hand's gesture tells you what it will do | No conventional level kit, almost no colour signalling, costly anatomy. One room per author drawing |
| 4 | **The timetable is the opponent; nobody is the villain** | Bells, fixed slots, a rank that slips while you stand still. Reality stays plausible; cracks are rare and small | No boss to defeat and no catharsis by victory. Repetition has to be felt without becoming dull |
| 5 | **What is not graded keeps you alive** | Rest, food, company and idleness restore you, are never scored, and are never called correct | No affection meters, no quest log for life, weak feedback by design. Optimising players may feel under-rewarded |

How each pillar steers the crafts: **story** is told by returns and by what changed on the page (4, 5). **Art** keeps reality loud and the Gray Space built of bodies (3, 4). **Play** rewards reading (1, 2). **Sound** follows the same split: reality has bells, voices and weather; the Gray Space has paper, graphite and breath, and no music that tells you how to feel **[Proposal]**. **UI** shows the school's measures as numbers and the student's state without numbers, as the retained HUD already does (5). **Characters** are people inside the same machine, never its agents (4).

## 5. The core loop at three scales

1. **Encounter** (minutes): look → form an idea → perform an operation → the body changes truthfully → answer, force, or leave.
2. **Day** (a play session): fixed slots you do not own → a question arrives → Gray Space → return, later than you left → one or two slots you do own → spend them on what is scored or what is not.
3. **Term** (the whole game): evaluation → rank on a moving cohort → sanity trend → what the world shows you → what you choose to do with free slots.

Proposed drift across the game, as structure only, not plot **[Proposal]**:

| Stage | The switch | What the return changes |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Only at a question. Reads as "what thinking feels like" | Nothing but the clock and the page |
| Middle | Also at moments of evaluation (a ranking posted, a remark) | Small carry-overs: one object, one gesture seen in daylight |
| Late | Without a question at all; a bell is heard inside the Gray Space | Reality narrows with sanity; the two places quote each other |
| End | The timetable stops. There is an afternoon with no slots | What the player does with it is the ending. The game does not grade it |

Candidate correspondences between the two worlds, **for the author to accept or reject**, since no meaning is canonical: an exam ↔ a sequence of tables; a teacher's judgement ↔ a pointing hand (pointing hands appear in three of the four drawings **[Observed]**); a ranking ↔ limbs stacked in order.

## 6. Two routes

Both keep every fixed requirement. They differ in what the player's hands do.

| | **Route A · The Table** | **Route B · The Paper** |
|---|---|---|
| One line | One question is one room. You walk in it and operate on it | One exam is one scene. Questions come to you in turns and you triage |
| Player action | Move, study, push, join, set against, answer, leave. Real time at walking pace, no reflex combat | Each turn, under a countdown: examine, try, use a named method, skip |
| How solving is embodied | You *perform* the method on physical parts; the world shows the consequence | You *recognise* the type and pick the method; an animation resolves it |
| Rhythm | 3 to 6 minutes per question; deep, few | 20 to 60 seconds per question; quick, many |
| Story delivery | The room itself and what the return shows on the page | The order of the paper, the marks afterwards, the rank slip |
| Visual staging | Third-person inside the drawing; an authored view on entry and whenever you study | Fixed authored camera that can match a drawing exactly; limbs can be layered planes |
| Production cost | Higher per room (walkable anatomy, collision, camera). Fewer rooms and types needed | Lower per scene. Much more content needed (many questions), heavier menus |
| Chief risk | It may read as a puzzle room rather than a fight; 3D readability of limbs | It becomes a menu quiz, and resembles familiar turn-based school games |

**Route A, one concrete sequence.** Evening study at a long table. A worksheet question on two conditions sharing one unknown → the room drains to grey; the table is now vast, two arms hold two trays, a small hand pinches a lid shut → you study, join the trays, the two opposing hands clasp and withdraw, you give the answer, the pinch lets go → you are back at the table 25 minutes later; one clean line is on the page in the student's hand → one free slot before the bell: the second question, or the tea going cold beside you → next morning the sheet is collected; the school sees one answer and one blank, and someone at the table saw how you worked.

**Route B, one concrete sequence.** Weekly quiz in the classroom. The bell → the desk stays, the room falls away; six questions are brought to the desk one by one by a hand → you examine the first and recognise it, one method ends it; the third is unfamiliar, two tries cost health, you skip; turns run out at the fifth → the paper comes back with red marks; a rank slip → one free slot: copy a classmate's corrections (learn the type) or go and eat → next quiz the learned type takes one move, and your rank has moved anyway because everyone else's did.

## 7. Recommendation

**Build Route A for the encounter, and keep Route B's triage as the later shape of an exam.**

Reasons:
1. The project's claim is that *executing* a method can be play. Route B cannot test that claim; Route A is the only place it is proved or disproved.
2. Route A needs few question types. Route B needs many questions before it feels like anything, which is content the project cannot yet afford.
3. Route A honours the confirmed third-person form and the bodily, spatial grammar of the drawings.
4. Borrow from B now: the authored camera on entry and while studying, and a deliberate pace with no reflex test. Borrow later: an exam as a row of tables under one clock, where leaving a table is the triage.

One thing the owner should know: an earlier instruction of theirs asked for turn-based fights with a countdown each turn. The current baseline leaves this open. Route A is a departure from that earlier wish; Route B is its descendant. This is the owner's choice.

**Do not build yet:** a timetable system; rank simulation; sanity effects beyond one demonstration; more than one question type; a method tree; named characters or plot; a second Gray Space room; generators or tools; saving; the final encounter HUD; two language versions.

## 8. Critique of the addition-elimination candidate

**Verdict: keep the method, replace the embodiment.**

What is right: "two opposites cancel when joined" is an excellent first method. It can be *seen* without algebra, and the data-driven second instance is good discipline.

What does not work as written:
1. **It is a multiple-choice question.** Four terms, pick two, press Q. There are six possible pairs and one is accepted. Matching `+y` to `−y` is glyph matching, which is what the brief itself fears: selecting an answer and watching an animation.
2. **The world does not answer a wrong idea.** A wrong pair returns a message and changes nothing. But adding two equations is always legal; it simply may not help. Refusing it teaches that the game checks answers, not that operations have consequences.
3. **Only one road exists.** Subtracting the two conditions removes x and gives y, from which x follows. It is longer and still correct. Allowing it is what makes understanding *cheaper* rather than *required*.
4. **The enemy is a label.** Two lines of text on a shield is a question with a health bar, not a body derived from structure.
5. **The pressure is unrelated.** A ground slam on a timer could belong to any enemy in any game. Pressure should come from what this question is: two conditions that will not be brought together.
6. **The finish is a leftover.** Walking up to strike an exposed core restores "attack until dead" for the last second.

The revision is specified in `FIRST_SLICE_DESIGN.md`: the two conditions are two trays held by two arms; the unknown is a covered cup, the same on both; the `y` term is a hand pressing down on one tray and an identical hand pushing up under the other. Joining, setting against, guessing and leaving are all possible and all truthful. Its numerical tuning is not preserved.

## 9. Proposed changes to fixed requirements

These are offered separately and assumed nowhere else.

1. **Questions are never creatures.** A question is an *arrangement held in place by hands*. The baseline allows personified or objectified; I propose always objectified, with the hands as the only bodies. This keeps the drawings' vocabulary and removes a bestiary from the budget.
2. **A question is answered, not killed.** Victory is the moment the hand lets go. It is still a contest that hurts.
3. **One room per author drawing.** Every Gray Space room begins from a drawing by the owner. This bounds scope and keeps the owner's hand in the game.
4. **The student keeps a trace of reality's colour in the Gray Space**, as the only saturated thing there. It solves player readability without coloured markers. A matter of taste for the owner.
